---
title: "Deliberate Divergence"
description: "Some things are moved away from what the declaration says, on purpose, and the next plan has no way to know that."
---

A stack's declared source says what a resource should be. Almost
everything `ubx` does moves a resource toward that.

A few things deliberately move one away. [Restoring](/concepts/restore) to
an earlier head does, and so does reverting observed [drift](/concepts/drift)
back to what the ledger already believed. Afterwards the declaration and
the ledger disagree, on purpose, and the system has no record that the
disagreement was chosen rather than accidental.

That residue is what this page is about, because it is where an otherwise
sound design quietly loses the thing it just did.

## The restore that gets undone by the next routine plan

Once a restore ships, the ledger and live state hold the earlier shape.
The declaration still describes the newer one.

So the next plan resolves the declaration, finds live state differs, and
proposes changing it back. Correctly. Every step is behaving exactly as
designed, and the outcome is that a restore someone shipped on purpose is
reverted by a plan nobody thought twice about.

What makes it dangerous is not that the plan is wrong. It is that the plan
looks completely ordinary. The person running it was expecting nothing to
happen, which is precisely the state of mind in which a diff gets skimmed
and accepted.

## Why this is not called "restore"

Restore is the case that made the gap visible. It is not the concept.

Drift revert leaves the identical residue for the identical reason: it
sets a resource to something the declaration does not describe, and the
next plan proposes undoing it. Anything else that ever moves a resource
away from its declaration will do the same.

Naming the mechanism after the command that exposed it would have meant
building it a second time the first time someone noticed the other case.
So it is named for the property the cases share, and recognising a new one
later is a matter of classifying it rather than of building anything.

## It reports a fact and refuses to draw the conclusion

The tempting message is "this plan would undo your restore". That cannot
be said safely.

A plan touching one attribute of a restored resource is not necessarily
reversing the restore. Several ordinary changes later, the claim is a
guess dressed as a fact. And moving forward again after a restore is a
completely normal thing to do, so a marker that treated it as a mistake
would be wrong more often than right.

What gets said instead is always true: the most recent change to this
resource was a restore to a particular head, at a particular time. A
reader who meant to move forward reads it and continues. A reader who did
not now knows before accepting rather than afterwards.

This is a deliberate choice about what a warning is for. An overclaiming
marker on a legitimate workflow is exactly how people learn to skip
warnings, and this one has to keep working after being read many times.

## Nothing new is recorded

A restore already carries a reference to the head it reproduced, recorded
as evidence. A drift revert already announces itself in its own proposal
kind. Both facts were in the ledger already; nothing was looking at them.

So this is detection and rendering rather than a schema change, which is
unusual for something in this area and worth noting: the ledger had
recorded enough, and the gap was in the reading.

## What "currently diverged" means, exactly

The question is not "has this resource ever been restored". It is "is a
deliberate departure what this resource holds right now", and the
difference matters in two ordinary cases.

A restore superseded by a later ordinary change is no longer the answer.
Warning about it would tell someone that a value set yesterday was chosen
deliberately last week, which is false.

An accepted but never shipped restore has set nothing at all. There is
nothing deliberate about the resource's current state yet.

Both fall out of building this on the same fold that computes a resource's
current state and [provenance](/concepts/provenance), rather than on a
search back through history for the most recent restore. The fold already
knows which operation last contributed; asking it for the proposal rather
than the value is a third reading of one walk.
