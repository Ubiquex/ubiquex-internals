title: "Restoring an Earlier Head"
description: "Going back is a proposal like any other, not a rewind, and naming which head to go back to is a separate problem with a separate answer."
---

"Put it back the way it was last Tuesday" is the oldest request in
infrastructure, and the one most systems answer badly. Usually by
rewinding: undoing entries, rolling back a state file, treating history
as something that can be edited to make the present acceptable.

`ubx` cannot do that, because the [ledger](/concepts/ledger) is
append-only and a signature covers a chain. Rewinding would mean
invalidating everything downstream of the point you rewound to, which is
the same as saying the record was never trustworthy.

So going back is not a rewind. It is a proposal.

## A restore is an ordinary proposal that happens to look backwards

`ubx restore <head>` reads an earlier head, reconstructs the shape the
stack had at that point, and resolves it against **current** reality. The
result is an ordinary change proposal: a delta, a blast radius, a cost
estimate, and a signature. It goes through
[acceptance](/concepts/proposal) like anything else, and changes nothing
until it is accepted and shipped.

The ledger grows forward. What was there before stays there, including
whatever you are restoring away from. Six months later the record still
shows the change, the restore, and who signed each.

### Exact state, not a merge

The semantics are deliberately blunt, because the alternative is a system
that quietly decides what you meant:

- a resource the target head had, that current state does not, is
  **created**
- one both have, with different config, is **modified** back to the
  target head's recorded value
- one current state has, that the target head does not, is **destroyed**

That last case is the one worth stating plainly. A resource created after
the target head is destroyed, unconditionally. Restore does not preserve
it on the theory that you probably wanted to keep it, because a restore
that silently keeps some things is not a restore of anything in
particular.

Every destroy appears in the blast radius, and destroys require explicit
confirmation at acceptance. That protection already existed for every
destructive proposal, and restore gets no exemption from it. This is a
recurring shape in the system: rather than a special safety mechanism for
a dangerous new operation, the dangerous new operation is expressed in
terms the existing safety mechanism already covers.

### Resolved against now, not copied from then

The reconstruction is of the target head's **config**, not of its
outcome. Resolving happens fresh, against live reality, through the
ordinary resolver path. So the blast radius and cost delta are real
rather than historical, and a resource that has drifted since shows up as
the modify it genuinely is.

[Cross-stack references](/concepts/cross-stack-references) are the
interesting case here, and they need no special handling. By the time a
`$cross` marker reaches a recorded proposal's applied state it is already
a resolved literal; the marker is gone. Restore replays the literal
exactly. A neighbour stack having moved on since is irrelevant, because
restore never re-resolves the reference at all.

### Restoring a restore is not a special case

A ledger can hold several proposals each claiming to restore some earlier
shape. Nothing in the fold cares. Walking the chain as of an arbitrary
head does not ask whether an intermediate proposal was itself a restore,
and there is no branch anywhere that does.

That falls out of restore being an ordinary proposal rather than an
operation on history. A mechanism that rewound would need to answer what
happens when you rewind past a rewind. This one never poses the question.

### Why drift revert is not a smaller version of this

They look adjacent and are not.
[Drift revert](/concepts/drift) is resource-scoped, modify-only, and
always targets whatever the ledger currently believes. Restore is
stack-scoped, uses all three delta shapes, and targets an arbitrary
earlier head.

Reverting one resource's observed drift is a different job from
reproducing a whole stack's earlier shape, and the narrower mechanism was
built for the narrower job rather than as a first step toward this one.

### The link back is evidence, not a pin

A restored proposal records `{kind: "restore", ref: <target head>}` in
its sources, and `ubx why` renders it as "restored ledger head
`<short id>`".

That is a historical fact, never re-validated. It cannot go stale: a past
head is immutable, and the stack diverging further afterwards has no
bearing on the claim that this proposal restored that head. The same
posture [promotion](/concepts/proposal) already takes with its own
provenance link, and the same distinction
[provenance](/concepts/provenance) draws generally between recording what
was decided and constraining what happens next.

## The restore that succeeds and is then undone

Restore reconstructs recorded configuration. It never re-runs a
blueprint, and nothing it rebuilds consults what the stack declares
today.

That is correct, and it opens a gap that is entirely invisible from either
side. A stack whose table names a blueprint at `v2`, restored to a head
that used `v1`, ends up holding `v1`'s shape in its ledger and `v2`'s
declaration in its config. The next plan reads the table, expands `v2`,
and proposes moving everything back.

Both steps behave exactly as designed. The net effect is that nothing
happened, and nothing in either step said so.

So the restore receipt compares the two and reports where they differ.

### The unit is everything the stack declares, not a version

A stack declares several blueprints and routinely does, so this is not
"which version moved". Restoring means reconciling all of them against
what the head actually used, in three directions: a declaration that
moved, one the head used that the stack no longer has, and one the stack
has that the head never used.

The second is the one that makes the problem concrete. A stack drops a
blueprint, later restores to a head that used it, and what is needed is a
declaration **added back**. There is no version to move.

### A declaration lives in either of two places

Saying "the table" was wrong, and wrong in the way that produces bad
advice rather than an incomplete answer.

A stack can declare a blueprint in `.ubx/config`'s `blueprints` table, or
**inline on the HCL block itself**, with `source`/`version`/`path` and no
table entry at all. Restore never sees the HCL file: it is handed a head,
not a document.

So a report reading only the table told every inline-declaring stack that
every one of its blueprints was missing, advising a reader to add an entry
already present a few lines above in their own file.

What restore can see is `.ubx/blueprints.lock`, which records what each
remote call resolved to, keyed by stack and name, wherever the declaration
was written. That is what makes an inline declaration visible here, and it
is also the better predictor of what the next plan will do.

A local inline call appears in neither and is correctly never reported. It
records no declaration at all, because a path is not a reference that can
be repointed and there is no entry behind it to reconcile.

### Two names for one blueprint, and what reconciles them

The two sides do not agree on what a blueprint is called, and nothing
says they should.

A ledger reference carries the blueprint's own **packaged** name, from
its build-time record. A lock entry is keyed by the name the **call**
used, derived from the source's last path segment. An OCI repository
called `rev-bp` can hold a blueprint packaged as `bp`, and both names are
right: the first answers "what is this blueprint" and belongs in
provenance, the second answers "what did this stack ask for" and belongs
in a file a person edits.

Pairing them on content hash is the obvious move and it fails at exactly
the wrong moment. Two versions of a blueprint are different bytes by
construction, so a version change is precisely when the hashes do not
match, both sides fall back to names that disagree, and one blueprint is
reported twice. The hash worked only when there was nothing to say.

The key that survives a version change is the **identity of the source**
with its version stripped, which is the same derivation the lock already
uses for its own key: `oci://host/repo:v1` and `oci://host/repo:v2`
produce one value. The content hash stays as a last resort, for identical
bytes published under two spellings, where it can still add a pair and can
no longer be the reason one is missed.

### It reports and never edits, because of where the hard case falls

The effective table is merged across a cascade of config directories, and
the cascade already tracks which file supplied each effective value.

So an entry that already exists has a known home: changing or removing it
is a well-defined edit. An entry that is **absent** has no such record,
because nothing supplied it. Adding a dropped blueprint back is a decision
about which file in the cascade should own it, and nothing but a guess
selects one.

The case that motivates the feature is therefore precisely the case an
editor could not answer. An editor handling the two it could while
refusing the third would be worse than reporting all three, so it reports
all three and writes nothing, and says why rather than leaving the silence
to read as an oversight.

### What it compares is usage, not the historical table

Worth being exact, because the two are easy to conflate.

The ledger records what produced each resource, never what was declared.
Those come apart in both directions. A blueprint declared but never called
leaves no trace at all, so it cannot appear. And one name can carry two
different sources at a single head, when a resource created under `v1` was
never re-resolved after the table moved to `v2`.

The second is reported as a conflict rather than resolved by picking a
winner. No single entry reproduces resources that genuinely came from
different sources, and choosing one would make the report wrong rather
than merely incomplete.

### Two limits it states about itself, every time

**Arguments are not checked, and what that means depends on how the
blueprint was called.** The same blueprint at the same version can produce
a different plan when its arguments change, and this report does not
compare them.

For a blueprint called from HCL the arguments **are** in the ledger now,
recorded alongside the declaration, so this is a limit on the report
rather than on the record. For one called directly from an SDK program
they are not recorded at all: the arguments are ordinary locals inside the
evaluated program and never cross into the host, which would take a change
to three separately published runtimes to fix.

An earlier version of this page said arguments were simply not recorded in
any language. That was true when it was written and stopped being true the
same week, which is worth noticing about the sentence itself: a claim that
something cannot be known is a claim about the world, and it goes stale
exactly like any other.

That is a likelier cause of a divergence than anything the report does
check, which is why it is stated on every run including a clean one. On a
clean run it is the only remaining explanation, so that is where saying it
matters most. A report implying "match these and you are back" would be
worse than no report.

**A head resolved before declarations were recorded can only be partly
checked.** A content hash names bytes and cannot be reversed into a
source, so for those entries the report says the source is unknown and
names them as unchecked rather than counting them as matching. Every
ledger written before declarations existed is in this state, and a thin
answer that reads as a clean bill would be the worst way to deliver it.

## Naming a head, which is a separate problem

A proposal is identified by a content hash. That is right for a machine
and poor for a person, and it is most poor exactly where it matters most:
restoring to an earlier head means naming one, so the whole interaction
is built around pasting a hash.

`ubx alias set` assigns a human-readable name to a head, with `resolve`,
`list` and `remove` alongside it. Restore takes an alias anywhere it
takes a head.

### An alias is a lookup, never an identity

The hash remains what gets signed and what the chain is built on. An
alias points at a hash and is stored entirely outside the ledger and
outside the hashed proposal shape.

So adding, changing, or removing one cannot invalidate a signature and
never touches the proposal it names. `ubx alias remove` deletes a
pointer, not a head.

This is the same discipline restore and promotion apply to their own
provenance links, taken a step further. Those are additive facts recorded
*inside* a proposal. An alias is a pointer recorded entirely *outside*
one, which is why it can be edited freely while a proposal cannot.

Repointing an existing alias requires `--force`. A name that silently
starts meaning something else is worse than no name, particularly for a
name someone is about to hand to a restore.

### Workspace-local, deliberately

Aliases live in `.ubx/aliases.json`, beside `.ubx/config.hcl`, and not in
the ledger store.

The reason is structural rather than stylistic. A ledger backed by a
remote store has no local directory of its own; only the git-directory
store does. Addressing aliases through the store interface would mean
adding alias methods to every store backend, for something that is
fundamentally a human-managed, git-shared artifact rather than ledger
content.

So the workspace directory that already holds the config holds the
aliases too, regardless of what backs the proposals themselves.
